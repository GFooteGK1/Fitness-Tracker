## What Changed

- **New experts discovered**: Joshua Pelland and Michael Zourdos added continuous dose-response work; Jozo Grgic and James Nunes added exercise-order evidence; J. Matt McCrary added warm-up evidence; Moritz Schumann added concurrent-training evidence; John Kiely added a direct critique of rigid periodization; Xing Zhang and Jonathon Weakley added time-efficiency evidence.
- **Shifted positions**: The earlier doctrine's broad volume guidance is now more precise: weekly volume has diminishing returns, strength frequency may have an independent but diminishing benefit, and hypertrophy frequency is mainly a distribution choice when volume is equated.
- **Confidence changes**: Confidence increased that full-body and split routines are interchangeable when weekly work is equated, that priority work belongs early in the session, and that a fixed exercise count is not an evidence-based definition of completeness.
- **New divergence**: This review adds meaningful disagreements around fixed versus responsive periodization, short-rest density versus output-preserving recovery, and universal set targets versus individualized dose-response.
- **Temporal shifts**: Weekly dose is tagged SHIFTING rather than STABLE because the 2026 continuous dose-response analysis improves the model while retaining substantial uncertainty at higher volumes.

## Executive Summary

A high-quality plan is complete when it supplies the athlete's required weekly exposures and every session delivers a named priority adaptation within the athlete's time and recovery budget. The evidence does not support a universal minimum number of exercises per session, nor does it make a body-part split inherently better than full-body training when weekly work is equated. The strongest session-order consensus is specific preparation followed by the highest-priority skill, speed, power, or strength work before fatigue, with secondary and assistance work included only when each fills a defined weekly gap. Weekly volume, frequency, exercise selection, rest, conditioning placement, and progression should be individualized inside explicit bounds; complex periodization and generic hard finishers should not substitute for clear intent and review.

## Topic & Scope

**Original question**: "The current program is lacking in completeness. The sessions feature a generic warm up followed by one movement. We need to nail down the movement recommendations and overall ability to develop a high quality training plan first."

**Scope decisions**: This review narrows the prior twelve-domain doctrine to the construction of complete eight-week plans, complete training weeks, and complete sessions for generally healthy adults training two to six days per week. It covers strength, hypertrophy, power, speed, aerobic and anaerobic conditioning, resilience, and mixed goals. It does not prescribe rehabilitation, disease-specific exercise, return-to-play decisions, or elite sport peaking without the athlete's sport calendar and multidisciplinary context. "Complete" is evaluated by adaptation roles and weekly coverage, not visual complexity or a fixed movement count.

## Convergence Points

### CP-1: Completeness is role and coverage based, not exercise-count based

- **Confidence**: 4/4 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: A session is not incomplete merely because it has few movements, and it is not complete merely because it has many. It is complete when it delivers the day's adaptation and the exposures assigned to that day from the weekly plan.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Brad Schoenfeld](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v1i1.81), [Ramos-Campo and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/), [Joshua Pelland and Michael Zourdos](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)
- **Key evidence**: ACSM reports that many resistance-training forms work and focuses recommendations on adaptation-specific load, volume, frequency, range, and intent. Split and full-body routines produce similar strength and hypertrophy when weekly work is equated. Dose-response evidence is expressed in weekly sets and exposure, not a mandatory number of exercises per session.

### CP-2: Prepare specifically, then place priority work first

- **Confidence**: 4/4 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Preparation should rehearse the positions, range, rhythm, or output used in the main work without creating fatigue. The highest-priority strength, skill, power, or speed exposure should follow while performance quality is most available.
- **Supporting experts**: [J. Matt McCrary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25694615/), [Jozo Grgic / James Nunes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Timothy Suchomel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z)
- **Key evidence**: Dynamic, task-relevant warm-ups can improve subsequent strength and power. Strength gains are greatest for exercises placed early, regardless of whether the exercise is single- or multi-joint; hypertrophy is less order-sensitive. The implication is priority-first rather than a universal compound-before-isolation rule.

### CP-3: Set weekly dose before choosing the split

- **Confidence**: 4/4 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Weekly volume has a positive dose-response with diminishing returns. Frequency is used to distribute that work, preserve quality, increase specific practice, and fit the athlete's schedule; the split itself is not the adaptation.
- **Supporting experts**: [Joshua Pelland and Michael Zourdos](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Ramos-Campo and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/), [Brad Schoenfeld](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v1i1.81)
- **Key evidence**: The newest meta-regression finds diminishing returns for volume, stronger diminishing returns for strength, negligible volume-adjusted frequency effects for hypertrophy, and a positive but diminishing strength-frequency relationship. A 2024 meta-analysis found split and full-body routines equivalent when volume was equated.

### CP-4: Every movement should fill a named gap

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Exercise selection should serve specificity, muscle or regional coverage, movement skill, equipment, preference, and tolerance. Multi-joint work is efficient, while single-joint, unilateral, trunk, landing, and resilience work can be valuable when they fill a specific need.
- **Supporting experts**: [Brad Schoenfeld](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v1i1.81), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Timothy Suchomel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z)
- **Key evidence**: Different equipment and exercise types can produce useful adaptations. Hypertrophy recommendations support varied angles and a combination of movement types for complete development, while strength and power retain stronger specificity requirements. Evidence does not support adding assistance work without a stated coverage purpose.

### CP-5: Rest should preserve the intended output

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Rest is not a generic clock setting. It should be long enough to reproduce the required force, velocity, technique, or target-muscle work, and shorter only when density or incomplete recovery is part of the intended conditioning stimulus.
- **Supporting experts**: [Alec Singer and Brad Schoenfeld](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23539308/)
- **Key evidence**: Hypertrophy can occur across many rest periods, with a small advantage for rest longer than 60 seconds and uncertainty beyond roughly 90 seconds. Strength, power, and interval work require enough recovery to preserve the target output. Short rest is a tool, not evidence that a session is harder or better.

### CP-6: Power and speed are protected exposures

- **Confidence**: 4/4 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Power and speed work use maximal intent, appropriate loading, and enough recovery to preserve output. They belong early and end when speed, mechanics, landing, or force direction meaningfully deteriorate.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Timothy Suchomel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z), [Thomas Haugen](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30963240/), [velocity-loss synthesis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9807551/)
- **Key evidence**: ACSM supports moderate loads moved with a fast concentric phase and low-to-moderate volume for power. Velocity-loss research shows that accumulating more within-set fatigue changes the adaptation and acute cost. Sprint practice remains task-specific and quality dependent.

### CP-7: Conditioning must name the variables it manipulates

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: "Hard conditioning" is not a complete prescription. A conditioning block must define modality, work intensity and duration, recovery intensity and duration, repetitions, series, and a repeatability or stop rule.
- **Supporting experts**: [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23539308/), [Moritz Schumann](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/)
- **Key evidence**: Buchheit and Laursen describe interval training as a multi-variable programming problem whose variables change metabolic and neuromuscular demand. Concurrent-training evidence shows placement also matters when explosive adaptation is a priority.

### CP-8: Mixed goals can coexist, but priority determines placement

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Aerobic and resistance work can coexist in a general-fitness plan without automatically erasing strength or hypertrophy. Same-session endurance becomes more concerning when explosive strength or speed is the primary adaptation.
- **Supporting experts**: [Moritz Schumann](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/), [Timothy Suchomel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z), [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23539308/)
- **Key evidence**: The updated concurrent-training meta-analysis found no meaningful interference for maximal strength or hypertrophy but identified reduced explosive-strength development when aerobic and strength training occurred in the same session. Sequence and separation should follow the lead goal.

### CP-9: Progression should be explicit, simple, and reviewable

- **Confidence**: 4/4 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Plans need progressive exposure and enough consistency to learn from results, but no complex periodization model should be treated as a universal biological law. Percentages, RIR, RPE, and velocity are complementary anchors and correction signals.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [John Kiely](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856877/), [autoregulation meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/), [Joshua Pelland and Michael Zourdos](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)
- **Key evidence**: ACSM did not find periodization consistently decisive for healthy-adult outcomes. Standardized and autoregulated load prescription produce similar strength gains. Kiely's critique supports treating the plan as a structured hypothesis that is revised from the athlete's response rather than as a deterministic forecast.

### CP-10: Time efficiency should remove filler before quality

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: EMERGING
- **Summary**: Short sessions can still be useful when the priority adaptation and necessary coverage are protected. Supersets can compress compatible assistance work, but their higher perceived and metabolic cost makes them a poor default for priority speed, power, or high-force work.
- **Supporting experts**: [Xing Zhang and Jonathon Weakley](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39903375/), [Jozo Grgic / James Nunes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)
- **Key evidence**: The 2025 superset meta-analysis found similar chronic strength and hypertrophy outcomes with shorter sessions, alongside higher effort and metabolic stress. Priority-order evidence means compression should first target compatible non-priority work and optional volume, not the recovery needed for the main adaptation.

## Divergence Points

### DP-1: Universal weekly set targets versus individualized dose-response

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Methodological

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Use category thresholds such as roughly ten weekly sets per muscle group as a practical optimization anchor. | Model volume continuously, count indirect work fractionally, and expect diminishing returns with wide individual uncertainty. |
| Held by: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/), [Brad Schoenfeld](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v1i1.81) | Held by: [Joshua Pelland and Michael Zourdos](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/) |

- **Context dependency**: Category anchors are understandable starting points for healthy adults. Continuous dose-response is better for an adaptive system because it avoids turning a population average into a personal requirement. The app should use bounded starting ranges and revise from actual performance and recovery.

### DP-2: Fixed periodization versus responsive planning

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Methodological

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Pre-plan phases, loading waves, and deloads to sequence adaptations and manage fatigue. | Treat long-range structure as a provisional map and adjust from the athlete's response because fixed stress-response assumptions are weak. |
| Held by: traditional strength and conditioning models summarized by [Suchomel and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z) | Held by: [John Kiely](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856877/), with outcome uncertainty reflected by [ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/) |

- **Context dependency**: The product's eight-week structure and weeks 4 and 8 reviews are useful coordination points, not claims that every athlete requires the same unloading. The review should choose which stressor to reduce, preserve, or assess.

### DP-3: Short-rest density versus output-preserving recovery

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Scope-dependent

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Use short rest and supersets to increase density and reduce session duration. | Use longer rest to preserve force, velocity, volume, and technical quality. |
| Held by: time-efficient training approaches represented in [Zhang and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39903375/) | Held by: [Singer and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/), priority-output recommendations in [ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/) |

- **Context dependency**: Density can be a valid conditioning or time-efficiency goal. It should not silently replace the recovery required for maximal strength, speed, power, or repeatable high-quality hypertrophy sets.

### DP-4: Percentage prescription versus autoregulation

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Methodological

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Use recent 1RM or e1RM percentages to standardize load and progression. | Use RIR, RPE, or velocity to match the load and volume to the athlete's current capability. |
| Held by: load-specific recommendations in [ACSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/) | Held by: autoregulation researchers summarized by [Hickmott and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/) |

- **Context dependency**: Percentages are valuable starting anchors for stable, assessed lifts. RIR, RPE, velocity, and execution quality are correction signals. The product should combine them within deterministic bounds rather than requiring either method alone.

### DP-5: Same-session concurrent training versus separated exposures

- **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Nature of disagreement**: Scope-dependent

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Combine aerobic and resistance work when schedule and general-fitness adherence benefit. | Separate them when explosive strength, speed, or the quality of either exposure is the lead goal. |
| Held by: the broad compatibility finding in [Schumann and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/) | Held by: the explosive-strength subgroup in the same [meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/) and priority recommendations from [Suchomel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0862-z) |

- **Context dependency**: General health and mixed fitness can favor practical combination. Power and speed blocks should protect freshness and use separate sessions or days when the schedule permits.

## Recommendations

### R-1: Replace exercise-count completeness with a role-based session contract

- **Derived from**: CP-1 (4/4)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: A role can contain one or several movements. Required roles depend on the weekly plan; visual complexity is not the goal.

### R-2: Build a weekly coverage matrix before composing daily sessions

- **Derived from**: CP-1 (4/4), CP-3 (4/4), CP-4 (3/3)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Strength is tracked most specifically by trained movement; hypertrophy by muscle or region; speed, power, and conditioning by performance quality and exposure.

### R-3: Use six explicit block roles

- **Derived from**: CP-1 (4/4), CP-2 (4/4), CP-7 (2/2)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Specific preparation and priority adaptation are default requirements. Secondary adaptation, assistance/capacity, conditioning, and downshift are conditional or optional rather than mandatory filler.

### R-4: Make session order priority driven

- **Derived from**: CP-2 (4/4), CP-6 (4/4)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: "Compound first" is only a proxy. A high-priority single-joint movement may correctly precede a compound movement when the goal requires it.

### R-5: Replace the generic warm-up with movement-specific preparation

- **Derived from**: CP-2 (4/4)
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Evidence is stronger for acute performance than injury prevention. The app must not claim the warm-up prevents injury.

### R-6: Give every movement a catalog role and coverage tag

- **Derived from**: CP-4 (3/3)
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Catalog tags should include domain, movement pattern or region, equipment, skill, fatigue cost, impact, contraindication flags, and valid substitutions; they should not encode medical judgments.

### R-7: Prescribe rest from the target output

- **Derived from**: CP-5 (3/3)
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Exact times remain ranges. Athlete readiness to reproduce the target is the operational test.

### R-8: Protect speed and power from fatigue

- **Derived from**: CP-6 (4/4), CP-8 (3/3)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: The app can use visible speed or technique stops without requiring a velocity device; measured velocity is optional.

### R-9: Make every conditioning block fully specified

- **Derived from**: CP-7 (2/2)
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Pace, power, or heart-rate numbers require a tested baseline or deterministic policy. Otherwise use bounded RPE, talk-test, or repeatability guidance.

### R-10: Use a preserve-trim-remove hierarchy for time constraints

- **Derived from**: CP-10 (3/3), DP-3
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Preserve compressed specific preparation and priority work; trim optional volume; remove filler; use compatible supersets only after those decisions.

### R-11: Treat the eight-week plan as a reviewable hypothesis

- **Derived from**: CP-9 (4/4), DP-2
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Weeks 4 and 8 remain product-defined review checkpoints. The review chooses whether to reduce volume, intensity, impact, complexity, or nothing; it does not force an identical deload.

### R-12: Keep evidence, product policy, and accepted prescriptions separately versioned

- **Derived from**: CP-3 (4/4), CP-9 (4/4), all divergence points
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: A new paper may update the next policy version but cannot silently alter an accepted plan. The LLM may explain the reference but cannot invent numeric policy or activate a plan.
