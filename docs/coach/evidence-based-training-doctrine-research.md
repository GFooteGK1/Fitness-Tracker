# Center of Gravity Report: Evidence-Based Training Doctrine for an Adaptive Coach

> Research conducted: 2026-07-27
> Mode: autonomous
> Experts analyzed: 10

---

## Executive Summary

The center of gravity is simple, individualized training directed at a specific adaptation, with consistency valued above unnecessary complexity. The 2026 ACSM position stand found that resistance training reliably improves strength, hypertrophy, power, and physical function, while many advanced methods do not consistently improve results for healthy adults; it recommends matching load, volume, and movement intent to the goal [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/). Hypertrophy requires sufficiently hard work but not routine momentary failure, whereas power requires maximal movement intent and preserved speed rather than grinding repetitions [Brad Schoenfeld](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9935748/) [G. Gregory Haff](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/d8cfbfa7955544a78832822bbada99b7/nsca-position-statement-on-weightlifting.pdf). Conditioning should combine a large base of easier work with deliberately selected hard intervals, but the distribution must reflect the athlete, sport, and phase rather than a universal ratio [Stephen Seiler](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/) [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23539308/). The coach should treat nutrition, sleep, subjective readiness, technique, and progressive tissue exposure as part of programming, while refusing to diagnose injuries or let a single wearable or workload metric dictate the plan [Louise Burke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/) [Shona Halson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31093921/) [Franco Impellizzeri](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534945/).

## Topic & Scope

**Original question**: "Topics should by Power, Hypertrophy, Speed, Explosiveness, Aerobic conditioning, Nutrition, Pre-hab/resiliance, Strength. anything I am forgetting?"

**Scope decisions**: Power and explosiveness are treated as one closely related adaptation family. Speed includes acceleration, maximum velocity, deceleration, and change of direction. Four missing operating domains were added: anaerobic work capacity; recovery and fatigue management; assessment and monitoring; and movement skill and technique. Adherence, concurrent-training interference, periodization, and medical safety are cross-cutting rules rather than separate athlete goals. The intended population is generally healthy adult athletes; rehabilitation, diagnosis, and return-to-play decisions remain outside the coach's scope.

## Convergence Points

### CP-1: Build the plan around the athlete and the adaptation

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Programming should start with the athlete's goal, history, constraints, preferences, and current ability. The plan should use the least complexity needed to create the intended adaptation and remain sustainable enough to follow.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf), [Martin Buchheit](https://hiitscience.com/hiit-questions-answered-martin/), [Gabriele Wulf](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/optimal-motor-learning/)
- **Key evidence**: ACSM emphasizes adherence and goal-specific settings; Buchheit emphasizes sport- and athlete-specific interval selection; Wulf shows that autonomy and enhanced expectations support performance and learning.

### CP-2: Strength favors heavy, technically sound practice

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Developing maximal strength generally benefits from repeated exposure to heavier loads, multiple quality sets, appropriate rest, and stable technique. Percentages are useful anchors, not commands that override daily performance.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf), [G. Gregory Haff](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/d8cfbfa7955544a78832822bbada99b7/nsca-position-statement-on-weightlifting.pdf)
- **Key evidence**: The ACSM synthesis highlights heavier loading around 80% 1RM or above and two to three sets when strength is the main outcome; Haff's position statement emphasizes sequential development, technical skill, and sufficient underlying force capacity.

### CP-3: Hypertrophy requires high effort, not routine failure

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Muscle growth can occur across multiple loading ranges when sets are sufficiently challenging and weekly work is adequate. Reaching momentary failure on every set is not necessary; lower-load work generally needs to finish closer to failure than heavier work.
- **Supporting experts**: [Brad Schoenfeld](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9935748/), [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf)
- **Key evidence**: The proximity-to-failure synthesis found no clear advantage for momentary failure and could not establish one universally optimal RIR; ACSM emphasizes higher weekly volume, around ten sets per muscle group as a useful optimization target, while treating failure as optional.

### CP-4: Power and explosiveness require speed to be protected

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Power work uses maximal acceleration intent against an appropriate load and ends before meaningful slowing or technical breakdown. High effort means trying to move fast; it does not mean accumulating failure-level fatigue.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf), [G. Gregory Haff](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/d8cfbfa7955544a78832822bbada99b7/nsca-position-statement-on-weightlifting.pdf), [Jean-Benoît Morin](https://libm.univ-st-etienne.fr/en/research-teams/spip/researchers/morin-jean-benoit.html)
- **Key evidence**: ACSM identifies moderate loads of roughly 30–70% 1RM moved as quickly as possible; Haff emphasizes force, rate of force development, and load selection across strength-speed and speed-strength; Morin links sprint performance to how effectively force is directed and expressed at speed.

### CP-5: Speed is a skill and a force-production quality

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Speed development requires fresh, high-quality sprinting or analogous fast movement, plus the force and technical qualities limiting that athlete. Acceleration, maximum velocity, braking, and direction change should not be collapsed into generic conditioning.
- **Supporting experts**: [Jean-Benoît Morin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22422028/), [G. Gregory Haff](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works/4631/)
- **Key evidence**: Morin identifies force orientation, contact characteristics, and force-velocity qualities as sprint determinants; Haff describes maximal strength as a foundation that supports higher power expression.

### CP-6: Conditioning needs both an aerobic base and selected hard work

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Summary**: Most conditioning volume should be tolerable enough to accumulate consistently, while harder intervals are chosen for a defined physiological or sport demand. A fixed 80/20 split is a useful historical pattern, not a universal prescription.
- **Supporting experts**: [Stephen Seiler](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/), [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23539308/)
- **Key evidence**: Seiler documents the durable pattern of large low-intensity volume plus limited high-intensity work; Buchheit describes interval prescription as the interaction of work intensity, duration, recovery, modality, repetitions, and series rather than simply "go hard."

### CP-7: Nutrition should support energy availability and the work being asked

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Adequate energy and protein support adaptation, while carbohydrate availability becomes increasingly important as intensity and duration rise. Supplements remain secondary to a sound food and fueling plan.
- **Supporting experts**: [Louise Burke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42070893/), [Stuart Phillips](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376)
- **Key evidence**: Burke's 2026 debate synthesis supports carbohydrate availability for demanding endurance performance and the 2023 IOC consensus treats low energy availability as a health and performance concern; the Phillips-led meta-analysis found that protein supplementation can augment resistance-training gains, with diminishing returns around 1.6 g/kg/day for many healthy adults.

### CP-8: Monitor trends and the athlete's report, not one readiness number

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Readiness decisions should combine recent performance, training history, subjective fatigue or soreness, sleep, and physiological trends. Wearables can inform the conversation but should not silently rewrite training.
- **Supporting experts**: [Shona Halson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31093921/), [Franco Impellizzeri](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10432367/), [Martin Buchheit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24578692/)
- **Key evidence**: Halson documents limitations across athlete sleep-monitoring methods; Impellizzeri distinguishes external exposure from internal dose and response; Buchheit frames physiological monitoring around sensitivity, usefulness, and practical context.

### CP-9: Resilience comes from progressive capacity, not injury prediction

- **Confidence**: 3/3 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Strength, balance, landing, plyometric, and movement-control work can build capacity when progressively matched to the athlete. No single workload ratio can identify who will be injured, and pain or suspected injury requires a clinician rather than an algorithmic training fix.
- **Supporting experts**: [Franco Impellizzeri](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/), [G. Gregory Haff](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/d8cfbfa7955544a78832822bbada99b7/nsca-position-statement-on-weightlifting.pdf), [Shona Halson](https://www.acu.edu.au/research-and-enterprise/our-people/shona-halson)
- **Key evidence**: Impellizzeri identifies conceptual and mathematical weaknesses in using ACWR for injury control; Haff emphasizes staged skill and load progressions; multimodal injury-prevention evidence favors strength and neuromuscular components over stretching alone [Jeppe Bo Lauersen](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871).

### CP-10: Coaching cues should be brief, external, and autonomy-supportive

- **Confidence**: 2/2 experts | **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Summary**: Athletes learn better when cues focus on the movement outcome, feedback is limited to what matters now, and the athlete retains meaningful choice. Long anatomical lectures should not replace a clear intention.
- **Supporting experts**: [Gabriele Wulf](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/optimal-motor-learning/), [G. Gregory Haff](https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/d8cfbfa7955544a78832822bbada99b7/nsca-position-statement-on-weightlifting.pdf)
- **Key evidence**: Wulf's OPTIMAL framework combines external attention, autonomy, and enhanced expectations; the NSCA position statement recommends short cue words and feedback appropriate to the athlete's skill level.

## Divergence Points

### DP-1: Fixed deload weeks versus athlete-triggered deloads

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Scope-dependent

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Pre-plan lower-stress weeks every four to eight weeks to preserve structure and manage accumulated fatigue. | Deload only when performance, soreness, motivation, or recovery trends show a need; unnecessary unloading may reduce useful training. |
| Held by: [international strength and physique practitioner consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/) | Held by: [recent experimental and autoregulation literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13031491/) |

- **Context dependency**: Weeks 4 and 8 are defensible review checkpoints for this product, but the amount of unloading should respond to the athlete. A deload can reduce volume, intensity, impact, complexity, or some combination; it need not mean no training.

### DP-2: Percentage-based loading versus RPE/RIR autoregulation

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Methodological

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Use percentages of a measured or estimated 1RM to standardize progression. | Use RPE, RIR, bar speed, or performance to adapt the day's load. |
| Held by: [Stuart Phillips / ACSM](https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf) | Held by: [Eric Helms and RIR researchers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/) |

- **Context dependency**: Percentages work well as starting anchors when the max is recent and the lift is stable. RIR and performance are better correction signals when readiness varies, but athletes estimate RIR less accurately with lighter loads or when far from failure [RIR accuracy study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33337690/).

### DP-3: Polarized versus pyramidal or threshold-heavy conditioning

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Scope-dependent

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Concentrate most work at low intensity with a small high-intensity dose. | Include more moderate or threshold work when it matches event demands, available time, and athlete level. |
| Held by: [Stephen Seiler](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20861519/) | Held by: [recent individual-participant network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/) |

- **Context dependency**: The common ground is substantial easy work and controlled hard exposure. The exact distribution should follow the goal, training age, time budget, and response rather than a universal ratio.

### DP-4: Acute:chronic workload ratio as an injury-control rule

- **Temporal**: SHIFTING
- **Nature of disagreement**: Factual

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Keep acute workload within a proposed ratio band relative to chronic workload. | The ratio contains mathematical and causal problems and should not be used as an injury prediction or control rule. |
| Held by: [Tim Gabbett](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789704/) | Held by: [Franco Impellizzeri](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/) |

- **Context dependency**: Tracking what changed remains useful, but the app should show load trends and uncertainty rather than issue deterministic injury-risk claims.

### DP-5: High-carbohydrate availability versus low-carbohydrate adaptation

- **Temporal**: STABLE
- **Nature of disagreement**: Scope-dependent

| Position A | Position B |
|------------|------------|
| Match carbohydrate availability to high-intensity and prolonged performance demands. | Use carbohydrate restriction to emphasize fat oxidation or accommodate preference. |
| Held by: [Louise Burke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42070893/) | Held by: [Timothy Noakes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42070893/) |

- **Context dependency**: Low-carbohydrate approaches can increase fat oxidation, but carbohydrate remains the stronger default for high-rate energy demands. The coach should not prescribe restrictive diets when energy availability, medical status, or disordered-eating risk is uncertain.

## Recommendations

### R-1: Use twelve coach reference sections

- **Derived from**: CP-1 through CP-10
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Power and explosiveness share one section; adherence and safety are operating rules.

The reference should cover assessment and baselines; strength; hypertrophy; power and explosiveness; speed and change of direction; aerobic conditioning; anaerobic work capacity; nutrition; resilience and movement capacity; recovery and fatigue; motor learning and technique; and adherence. Safety boundaries apply to every section.

### R-2: Keep the eight-week structure, but make weeks 4 and 8 review-led deloads

- **Derived from**: CP-1, CP-8, DP-1
- **Confidence level**: Moderate
- **Caveat**: Evidence supports deloading as a common fatigue-management practice more strongly than it supports one mandatory schedule.

Use weeks 1–3 and 5–7 to build the target qualities. At weeks 4 and 8, reassess performance and fatigue, then reduce the stressors that need relief. Preserve technique, movement exposure, and confidence unless the athlete's condition calls for more rest.

### R-3: Accept known 1RM, 3RM, or 5RM values and label all conversions

- **Derived from**: CP-2, DP-2
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Estimation error varies by exercise, athlete, and equation.

Store the lift, load, repetitions, date, whether the set was truly maximal, and athlete confidence. Convert 3RM or 5RM to a versioned estimated 1RM, display it as an estimate, and recalibrate it from later performance. Lower-repetition tests are generally more useful than high-repetition conversions, while direct 1RM testing is reliable only when appropriate and safely performed [1RM reliability review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7367986/) [exercise-specific estimation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11435939/).

### R-4: Prescribe intent before numbers

- **Derived from**: CP-1 through CP-5
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Numeric bounds remain necessary for validation and progression.

Every session should state the adaptation, target effort or quality, rest intent, stop condition, and what success feels like. Sets, repetitions, load ranges, duration, and pace are tools that implement that intent.

### R-5: Separate the stop rules by adaptation

- **Derived from**: CP-2, CP-3, CP-4
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Exercise selection and athlete experience affect the usable threshold.

Strength stops before unsafe technique or repeated grinding. Hypertrophy usually finishes near failure, often around one to two good repetitions remaining, without requiring failure on every set. Power, speed, and explosive work stop when velocity, distance, timing, or mechanics meaningfully degrade.

### R-6: Add anaerobic work capacity as a distinct conditioning domain

- **Derived from**: CP-5, CP-6
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: "Anaerobic" sessions still involve aerobic recovery and should be described by work demand rather than energy-system mythology.

Use interval duration, intensity, rest, movement mode, and repetition count to match the required repeat-sprint, glycolytic, or mixed-modal demand. Do not use "hard conditioning" as a complete prescription.

### R-7: Treat readiness as decision support

- **Derived from**: CP-8, CP-9, DP-4
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Missing or stale data should reduce confidence, not imply poor readiness.

Combine recent completion quality, soreness, sleep, motivation, stress, illness signals, and WHOOP trends. Explain which evidence changed the recommendation. Require athlete confirmation before multi-day plan changes.

### R-8: Keep nutrition within general-wellness boundaries

- **Derived from**: CP-7, DP-5
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Medical nutrition therapy, eating disorders, REDs assessment, and disease management require qualified professionals.

Coach adequate energy, protein, produce, hydration, and carbohydrate matched to the session. Avoid restrictive diet prescriptions and supplement claims. Escalate persistent low-energy, rapid weight change, menstrual disruption, recurrent bone stress, or disordered-eating signals.

### R-9: Coach technique with one outcome cue at a time

- **Derived from**: CP-10
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Internal anatomical cues remain useful when an external cue does not solve the problem.

Lead with the desired movement effect, give the athlete a small choice when safe, and keep feedback to the highest-value correction. Use video or more detail only when it improves understanding.

### R-10: Preserve the general-wellness boundary

- **Derived from**: CP-9
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: This is a product-safety boundary, not a substitute for legal or clinical review.

The coach should support healthy training and performance without diagnosing, treating, or claiming to prevent disease or injury [FDA general-wellness guidance](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-3-software-function-intended-maintaining-or-encouraging-healthy-lifestyle). Chest pressure, unusual shortness of breath, dizziness or confusion, extreme fatigue, or fast or irregular heartbeat should stop the session and trigger appropriate medical guidance [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/cardiac-rehab/getting-physically-active/develop-a-physical-activity-plan-for-you).

### R-11: Use a compact coaching response contract

- **Derived from**: CP-1, CP-10
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Safety explanations can exceed the normal length.

Default to: one action, one intent cue, and one stop or success condition. Add a short reason only when it changes athlete understanding. Avoid lectures, false precision, and unexplained plan changes.

### R-12: Version the doctrine and its evidence

- **Derived from**: all convergence and divergence points
- **Confidence level**: High
- **Caveat**: Stable guidance needs less frequent review than shifting findings.

Store a version, review date, supporting sources, confidence, and applicable population for every rule. Re-review SHIFTING findings at least annually and after major position statements.
